How to ask for a review
without making it awkward

Find the right moment and the right words to invite honest feedback after a great customer experience.

The LokalSterne guide·6 min read

Author

Adam Johnson

Product manager at Amazon

In this article:

  • Choose a natural moment
  • Keep the invitation simple
  • Give people an easy next step
  • Make room for honest feedback
  • Adapt the words to your business
  • Follow up with care
  • FAQ

Invite, don’t pressure.

A good review request makes it easy to say yes — and comfortable to say no.

You’ve just helped a customer, finished an appointment or served a lovely meal. Asking for a review can feel like turning a genuine conversation into a sales pitch. It doesn’t have to. The best invitation is a small, optional next step: thank the person, ask for honest feedback and show them where to share it.

Choose a natural moment

Wait until the service is complete and the customer has a little breathing room. At a shop, that might be after checkout. At a salon, it could be after the final mirror check. At a dental practice, wait until the patient is comfortable and any immediate questions have been answered.

Avoid asking while someone is in a hurry, uncomfortable or waiting for an unresolved issue to be fixed. Deal with their needs first. Offer the same opportunity to share feedback to customers generally, rather than asking only those you expect to leave a positive rating.

Keep the invitation simple

A short request sounds more natural than a rehearsed speech. Start with a thank-you, invite an honest account of the experience and make it clear that leaving a review is their choice. You don’t need to explain your whole marketing strategy.

Try: “Thank you for coming in today. If you have a moment, we’d appreciate an honest review of your experience.” Then pause. Avoid asking for five stars, offering a reward or watching over someone’s shoulder while they write.

Give people an easy next step

Use one direct review link or a clearly labelled QR code. Put it on a small card, a receipt or a follow-up message so the customer can choose when to use it. Explain what it opens; a plain “Share your experience” label is more helpful than a mysterious code.

Test the link on a phone before sharing it. Check that it opens the correct business profile and doesn’t send people through unnecessary steps. If someone prefers not to scan, offer a short link instead. Keep the request to one destination, not a list of platforms.

Make room for honest feedback

A review is the customer’s story, not a script you control. Welcome thoughtful criticism as well as praise. Don’t route only happy customers to public reviews or make a positive survey answer a condition for seeing the review link.

If someone mentions a problem, listen and help without making a review part of the resolution. For sensitive services such as dental care, remind people not to share private health information. When replying publicly, never confirm personal details they have disclosed.

Adapt the words to your business

Use these as starting points, not lines to memorise. Keep your usual tone and change the details to suit the visit.

Follow up with care

If you use email or a message after the visit, send it only where you have an appropriate basis to contact the person. Keep it relevant, include a direct link and respect their communication preferences. A polite invitation is enough; repeated reminders can make an optional request feel like an obligation.

AI can help you shorten or clarify your invitation, but check the tone before sending. Don’t use it to invent customer experiences or publish reviews on someone’s behalf. The customer should always choose what to say and whether to share it.

A thank-you, an honest invitation, one easy link.

That’s all you need to start. Pick one natural moment, practise a sentence that sounds like you and make the next step clear. Focus on making customers feel heard, not on collecting a particular rating.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time to ask for a review?

Right after the service is complete and the customer has had a positive moment, such as at checkout, after a final check or once their questions have been answered. Avoid asking while they are in a hurry or still waiting for a problem to be fixed.

Can I offer a discount or gift in exchange for a review?

No. Rewards for reviews can bias what people write and break the rules of most review platforms, including Google. Thank customers for their time instead and keep the request optional.

Should I only ask customers who seem happy?

No. Offer the same invitation to customers generally. Sending only satisfied customers to public reviews, or filtering people out with a survey first, is known as review gating and isn’t allowed on most platforms.

What should I do if a customer leaves a negative review?

Reply calmly and politely, thank them for the feedback and offer to continue the conversation privately. Don’t confirm personal details, and focus on fixing the issue rather than winning the argument.

How many times can I remind someone to leave a review?

One polite follow-up is usually enough. Repeated reminders turn an optional request into pressure, so respect people’s communication preferences and let them decide.

Is it okay to use AI to write review requests?

Yes, AI can help you shorten or clarify your invitation. Check the tone before sending, and never use it to invent customer experiences or post reviews on someone’s behalf.
